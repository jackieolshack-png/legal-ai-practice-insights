# Small Work. Strategic Value.
## Legal Work Allocation & AI Investment Decision Model

**Purpose:** Demonstrate a disciplined method for deciding whether a legal workflow is a good AI investment, which tasks should be augmented, which should remain with lawyers, and whether the future service model creates more value than the current one.

**Status:** Illustrative operating model inspired by Rachel Proffitt's public discussion. It is not a recommendation to buy or build a product, not a diagnosis of Cooley operations, and not a claim that an internal capability is missing.

## Executive thesis

The question is not simply whether AI can perform a task. The decision is how to allocate work across AI/self-service, junior associates, partners/senior lawyers, and hybrid arrangements to optimize client outcomes, lawyer development, relationship continuity, economics, and risk.

The unit of decision should be the **task or workflow component**, not the entire matter. A single client request can include AI-assisted intake, associate analysis, and partner judgment.

## Why this problem matters

Cooley's public October 20, 2026 press mention links to a Business Insider article dated October 19, 2026. The article reports Rachel Proffitt's discussion of:

- Clients valuing outcomes rather than the number of associate hours consumed.
- Cooley giving Y Combinator founders access to a Legora tool for reviewing common contracts without calling a lawyer.
- The risk of missing future client relationships if bots handle small initial needs without a pathway to human connection when a more consequential issue arises.
- The apprenticeship challenge if AI performs first drafts and issue spotting.
- Cooley exploring simulated legal matters and AI-powered mock depositions for associate development.

This public discussion provides the strategic problem context. It does not disclose Cooley's internal work-allocation process, routing rules, pilot metrics, or product capabilities beyond the examples described.

## Integrated use of the four frameworks

### 1. Problem Space → Solution Space

**Problem:** What client or business outcome is not being delivered well?

**Users:** Client, partner, associate, paralegal, business professionals, practice leadership.

**Alternatives:** How is the work done now? What existing self-service, tools, templates, people, and handoffs already work? Where are delays, rework, or missed connections?

**Business:** Why change—client experience, cost to serve, margin, capacity, relationship continuity, associate development, or risk?

**Validation:** What baseline evidence confirms the problem and its frequency?

Only then consider AI, process redesign, rules automation, human work, or a hybrid.

### 2. POWER

- **Possibility:** What might be improved, without presuming AI is the answer?
- **Opportunity:** Is the problem important enough to justify investment?
- **Workflow:** Which tasks, handoffs, decisions, and exceptions create the actual work?
- **Engineering:** Are data, systems, security, validation, integration, and support feasible?
- **Reflect:** Did the pilot improve the intended outcomes without degrading client service or lawyer development?

### 3. Eight evaluation lenses

Users; Business; Growth; Feasibility; Context; Evaluations; User Experience; Model Intelligence.

Use these lenses to prevent a technically impressive model from advancing when the user problem, business case, context, risk, or adoption case is weak.

### 4. Product delivery flow

Prioritization → roadmap → PRD → product backlog → sprint backlog → prototype/development → evaluation → reflect/scale/revise/stop.

Do not advance a candidate workflow to build simply because a prototype can be produced.

## Work allocation paths

| Path | Conditions that support it | Controls |
|---|---|---|
| AI / client self-service | Bounded, repeatable, low-consequence work with approved data and independent verification | Client choice, defined scope, human help, exception routing, approved environment |
| Associate-led | Meaningful developmental value; opportunity to practice reasoning, issue spotting, drafting, or client context | Supervising lawyer, feedback rubric, graduated responsibility; AI may be used after an independent first attempt |
| Partner / senior lawyer-led | Novel, high consequence, ambiguous, strategic, negotiated, or relationship-sensitive work | Named senior owner; client conversation based on actual need, not automated sales prompting |
| Hybrid | AI can handle defined preparation while a lawyer must interpret, validate, advise, or own the outcome | Task-level ownership, traceability, review, escalation and exception controls |
| Keep / redesign first | Weak data, low volume, unclear benefit, untestable outputs, unacceptable risk, or existing process already performs well | State why no AI investment is justified; revisit only when evidence changes |

## Worked example: common contract request from an early-stage client

This is a hypothetical workflow inspired by the public example of Cooley's Legora-enabled common-contract review access for Y Combinator founders. It is not a representation of Cooley's internal routing or a claim that any existing offering is deficient.

1. **Intake and information capture:** AI/self-service may structure required information in an approved environment. The client must have a clear path to human assistance.
2. **Standardized first-pass comparison or summary:** AI may organize information and surface candidate issues. A named associate or lawyer validates outputs against source material.
3. **Bounded legal analysis with developmental value:** An associate can own the first-pass analysis or complete an independent attempt before comparing against AI assistance. The supervising lawyer gives substantive feedback.
4. **Novel, high-consequence, or strategic issue:** Partner/senior lawyer owns the judgment and client conversation.
5. **Relationship continuity:** When the request indicates an additional client need, offer a relevant human conversation. Do not equate automation with loss of relationship or use an AI-generated upsell flag as the decision rule.

## Evaluation dimensions

Each candidate workflow is assessed across five value dimensions:

1. **Client:** outcome quality, speed, access, experience, trust, and clear escalation.
2. **Talent:** substantive associate reps, feedback quality, skill progression, and time to independent competence.
3. **Relationship:** continuity, access to a lawyer when appropriate, repeat instructions, and later consequential matters.
4. **Economics:** cost to serve, capacity released, redeployment, contribution margin, avoided cost, and recurring expense.
5. **Control:** confidentiality, professional responsibility, quality, error severity, explainability/traceability, and exception handling.

Feasibility gates include workflow stability, task volume, data availability/permission, independent verification, system integration, support ownership, and adoption friction.

## Baseline-versus-future business case

Baseline evidence should include task volume, cycle time, role-by-role touch time, cost to serve, quality, correction/rework, write-offs or agreed pricing, client feedback, partner involvement, and associate learning opportunities.

Future-state evidence should include AI processing time plus human review, exceptions, correction/rework, client experience, partner/associate/staff time, licensing and operating cost, integration, training, adoption, and new handoffs.

**Net released capacity (hours)** = annual task volume × adoption × (baseline human minutes − future human minutes) ÷ 60.

**Net economic value** = realized cost avoided + contribution from capacity actually redeployed − platform, integration, review, support, training, and change costs.

Do not label released capacity as cash savings unless spend is actually avoided or capacity is productively redeployed. Do not count speculative future matters as revenue. Track relationship and talent outcomes alongside financial results rather than forcing every benefit into a dollar figure.

## Pilot and decision discipline

- Establish baseline and representative sample before testing.
- Define expected outputs and material failure conditions with practice leaders.
- Compare current workflow with proposed allocation using the same task types and quality criteria.
- Measure quality, missed issues, false positives, human corrections, turnaround, cost, adoption, client experience, relationship continuity, and associate learning.
- Define release thresholds before the pilot; do not move the goalposts after seeing results.
- Scale only if incremental value over the current scenario is demonstrated and control requirements are met.

## Low-friction implementation principles

- Preserve existing document systems, matter data, permissions, templates, and approval points wherever feasible.
- Avoid duplicate trackers, duplicate data entry, unnecessary logins, and unclear handoffs.
- Pilot with a willing practice team and real workflow representatives, using appropriately approved/sanitized data.
- Give every AI output a named human owner where legal judgment is required.
- Train by role and task; collect user friction and revise the workflow before scaling.
- Provide exception paths and rollback.

## Roles and accountability

- **Practice leader / supervising lawyer:** owns legal judgment, client impact, acceptable risk, and associate-development intent.
- **Product / engineering:** owns solution design, technical feasibility, testing, integration, and operational support.
- **Program / transformation lead:** owns discovery discipline, cross-functional decision process, roadmap, dependency/risk management, adoption, metrics, and value realization cadence.
- **Finance:** validates cost, pricing, contribution-margin, and realization assumptions.
- **Risk / security / privacy / GC:** validates policy, data handling, confidentiality, privilege, and professional-responsibility controls.

## What this work sample claims—and does not claim

It demonstrates a proposed method for structured workflow discovery, work allocation, feasibility assessment, economic modeling, adoption planning, and outcome measurement. It does not claim Cooley has a problem, that any current tool fails, that the model has been deployed, or that financial or talent outcomes have been measured. All example routes and measures require validation with qualified practice, product, finance, and risk stakeholders.
